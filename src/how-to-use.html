Line Planner

← Back to the calculator

A line is your plan for one trainer fight: who leads, what it does each turn, and where the plan forks when the fight doesn't go to script. The planner draws that as turns joined by labelled arrows, and works out the fight state — boosts, status, hazards, weather — for every turn from the ones before it.

Starting a line

Lines live in the sidebar on the left of the Planner tab. New line asks for a trainer — type to filter, the list covers every trainer in the game — and everything after that hangs off which one you picked: their party, their sets, their AI flags, and whether the fight is a single, a double or a tag battle.

Click a line to open it. The × deletes it.

A line reads live from your Box, so levelling a Pokémon, teaching it a move or changing what it holds updates every line that uses it. Nothing is copied at the moment you build the plan.

Your Pokémon are identified by who they are, not by species, so a line survives evolution — the Machop you planned with is still the same entry once it's a Machoke.

Lines are saved in this browser, on this machine. Clearing site data loses them.

The workspace

Above the canvas: the toolbar, the opponent's AI flags, and the two party strips with your Box beside them.

Leader Roark
  • Add turn drops an empty turn onto the canvas. New turns are staggered so they don't land on top of each other.
  • All moves expands or collapses the move lists on every turn at once. Collapsing them is what keeps a long line readable.
  • Teams folds the party strips and the Box away to give the canvas more room. The choice is remembered.
  • Blind puts the numbers away: base power back on the moves, no health bars, no speed markers, and nothing struck through for being outsped. Working the fight out yourself is the part of a Nuzlocke that's actually the game, so the damage is there when you want it and gone when you don't.

    It switches the calculation off rather than hiding it, which matters — otherwise a move struck through would tell you it was a guaranteed KO just as plainly as the number would have. Everything that comes from the rules stays: hazards, screens, weather, status, trapping, Perish Song, and Fake Out both flinching and failing. The setting is remembered, and it's on until you turn it off.

AI flags

Straight from the game's own trainer data, in plain language on hover. These decide how much branching a fight actually needs: a Risky trainer will gamble on a low-accuracy move, so that fight wants a miss branch; a CheckHP trainer switches out when low, so planning a clean KO on it is optimistic.

    It's a range, because a damage roll is 85–100%: the solid part of the bar is what it holds even on the worst run of rolls, and the pale part is health it only has if the rolls went its way. Reading the solid edge alone is reading the plan pessimistically, which in a Nuzlocke is the right way to read it. The band widens with every hit — and narrows again at every branch, because a branch says which way the roll went.

    Know better? Put the real number in Health in the turn editor and the range collapses to it. That's also how you start a line mid-fight.

    End-of-turn effects resolve in this game's own order, and the order can decide a fight: Wish → weather → Ingrain and Aqua Ring → Leftovers → Leech Seed → poison and burn → Future Sight. A Pokémon that faints partway down that list never reaches the rest of it — so a sandstorm can finish something its Leftovers would have saved, and a Wish landing first can save something the sandstorm would have finished.

    Whether it says KO is measured against what the target has left, not against full — so it means "this finishes it here", and it means so even on the roll that treated the target best. For a move that hits 2–5 times it also means the fewest hits would do it, which is why those moves show such a wide range: two uncertainties stacked, the roll and the hit count. A range straddling the kill, like the one on the left, is the interesting case: it's a coin flip on the roll, and exactly what a You KO / You don't KO branch is for. Colour reads from your side: green is your KO, red is theirs.

Click to aim; click the same one again to go back to whoever happens to be across. A spread move says → both instead, since it has no choice to make.
  • Switch A real action for either side, and it costs that slot its turn — whoever comes in takes the hit. Switches resolve before moves.
  • Things worth checking Appears only when a turn has something odd about it, and hovering lists what. A Pokémon that's dead in the Box or already fainted on this branch, a move it no longer knows, a move that will simply fail — Leech Seed into a Grass type, or a Fake Out that isn't its user's first turn — a slot with no move chosen, or a branch the numbers say can't happen.

    Everything it flags is a certainty, never a hunch. Nothing is corrected for you, and nothing is blocked — a plan is allowed to be odd on purpose.

nd on a move — as in nd — means Platinum Kaizo has removed that move's self-inflicted stat drop. Superpower, Overheat, Psycho Boost, Draco Meteor and Leaf Storm have no drawback here, for you and for the AI.

The turn editor

Edit opens a dialog holding only what the card can't. Pokémon, moves and switches are all set on the card itself; this is for the state behind them.

It has one tab per Pokémon on the turn — both sides, and both slots each in a double — because everything in it belongs to one Pokémon rather than to the turn. Yours carry a blue underline, theirs red. The turn's note sits below the tabs, since that one really is the turn's.

Status is what that Pokémon carries into the turn — for walking in pre-slept or pre-poisoned. A Pokémon holds one non-volatile status at a time, so giving yours a harmless one is real protection against a trainer who leads with status. It carries down to every later turn, and — ends on this turn — is how you close a sleep out. It affects only the status; volatiles end on their own.

The level is editable. Click the Lv. on any Pokémon to say it levelled up. A long fight moves the numbers — something that levels on turn six of a gym leader has its stats change right then, so a plan drawn at the level you walked in with stops being true half way through. The change carries down to every turn below, because levelling up doesn't wear off, and it follows that Pokémon rather than the slot. Clear it to go back to what the Box says.

The planner won't work this out for you: it would need experience yields, who actually participated and the badge's level cap, and it would still be guessing at how the fight went. Evolution needs nothing — Pokémon evolve after a battle here, not during one, and a line already survives that on its own.

Move didn't go off says that Pokémon never got to act — you KO'd it first, it flinched, it was fully paralysed, or it missed. Its effects are skipped and its damage isn't taken off anybody, so outspeeding a lead genuinely denies the Stealth Rock instead of the planner laying it anyway. The move stays on the card and its damage figures stay visible, both struck through, so the plan still records what it was going to do.

You won't always need it. The planner works out turn order itself — priority bracket first, then speed — so if you outspeed something and your move kills it on every roll, it loses its move without you saying so. The same goes for a Fake Out flinch. What it will never do is guess: a kill that depends on the roll leaves the move landing, because that fork belongs on a You KO / You don't KO branch instead. Speed ties, Quick Claw holders are left alone for the same reason.

Trick Room is handled: while it's up, the slower Pokémon moves first inside each priority bracket — a Quick Attack still beats a Tackle, since only the speed comparison flips. It shows in the state bar at the top of the card. Two things about it are specific to this game: it lasts until the move is used again rather than running out after five turns, and it doesn't change the order of the turn it's cast on — this generation fixes turn order before anything moves, so the reversal starts on the turn after.

Stat stages correct the boosts by hand. They're added on top of whatever the turn inherited rather than replacing it, so +1 Atk after a Swords Dance reads as +3. Use it when a move's secondary-effect drop actually landed — the planner only models effects that are guaranteed — or when a line starts mid-fight with boosts already up.

Volatiles toggle on and off: confusion, Encore, Leech Seed, Disable, Torment, infatuation, Substitute. These sit alongside the status rather than replacing it, so a Pokémon can be poisoned and seeded at once, and they clear when it switches out. Taunt isn't offered because Platinum Kaizo removes the move entirely.

Moves apply these, but nothing ends them on a schedule. Encore runs 4–8 turns or until the encored move runs out of PP, Disable 4–7 — none of that is predictable, so the planner won't guess. Switch out, toggle it off on the turn it broke, or branch the early end, exactly as with sleep.

Setting up a whole split

Set up a split in the sidebar makes a blank line for every trainer between one gym leader and the next, in the order you fight them. That's the tedious part of starting a run — the Byron split alone is 69 fights, and none of them can be planned until the line exists. The button makes the shells; the thinking is still yours.

The number is how many lines it would still make — trainers you already have one for are skipped, so it's safe to press twice, and useful when you come back to a split you only half-planned. A tick means that split is fully laid out.

Which trainers belong to which split isn't in the game's own data — it comes from the community's Platinum Kaizo reference sheet, with thanks. Tag partners like Dawn/Lucas and Cheryl aren't included: they fight alongside you rather than against you, and the planner already knows about them from the battle format.

Filling a turn

Four ways in, because the party strips can be folded away and you shouldn't be forced to unfold them.

  • Drag a Pokémon from any strip onto a slot.
  • Click a slot — empty or filled — to pick from the right source for that slot.
  • Right-click a slot to empty it. An empty slot is a real state: after a faint, before the next one is in.
  • Switch on the move list, for a mid-fight change.

Slots know who owns them, so clicking one offers the right roster: your team for your slots, that trainer's party for theirs, the ally's party for a tag partner's slot.

Dragging a Pokémon onto empty canvas — not onto a card — creates a new turn there with that Pokémon already in it. It's the fastest way to start a line.

Branching

Every card has a blue connector nub hanging off its bottom edge. Drag it to say "and then":

  • Drop it on another turn to branch to that turn.
  • Drop it on empty canvas to create the next turn right there.

Nothing opens when you do. The new turn arrives empty and the branch starts as Then — "no condition" — so you can lay out the shape of a plan first and say what each path depends on afterwards. Click the arrow's label when you want to name it.

Arrows are coloured by what the branch means, so a line can be read at a glance without reading the labels:

  • went your way
  • went their way
  • a complication
  • no condition — just "then"

Click an arrow's label to change the condition, give it a custom label, or delete the branch. That is the only way to delete one — deleting isn't the consequence of clicking the most obvious target on the arrow.

Several branches can converge on the same turn. "You KO" and "They miss" both continuing into the same plan is normal, and each keeps its own label.

Conditions

Conditions are directional: a fight forks because of something you did or something they did, and "you miss" is a very different plan from "they miss". This is the full palette, as it appears in the branch editor.

Sacrifice is a planned trade: let something die to get a free switch-in. It's a legitimate move in a Nuzlocke, so it's a condition rather than something you have to describe in a note.

You wake / thaw and They wake / thaw exist because sleep and freeze break on their own schedule and can break on the very first turn. A branch is the honest way to plan around them; a turn count isn't.

A custom label overrides whatever condition is selected, for the cases the palette doesn't cover.

Folding

Fold on a card hides the turns that follow it, and the button becomes a count of what it's hiding. A folded card is drawn with a dashed amber border so you can see there's something behind it.

Folding hides only what is reachable exclusively through the folded turn. A turn that some other branch also leads to stays on the canvas, and isn't counted — so the number is what's genuinely hidden, not how many turns are downstream.

What the planner works out for you

Nothing about fight state is stored on a turn. It's recomputed by walking back up the graph, so editing one turn updates every turn after it, and rewiring a branch re-derives everything underneath it.

  • SLP 2 Sleep counts up to a guaranteed wake The number is turns elapsed, not a countdown. Sleep is pinned at both ends: a Pokémon can't wake on the turn it falls asleep, and after 4 turns it is awake whatever happens — the planner wakes it for you, so that doesn't need a branch. The turns in between are a genuine coin flip, so plan an early wake as a branch, or use "ends on this turn" to say it broke there.
  • PSN CNF One status at a time A Pokémon carries one non-volatile status, which is what makes pre-statusing your own Pokémon a real tactic. Volatiles like confusion sit alongside it.
  • Lum Berry spent Curing berries are spent once Roark's Cranidos holds a Lum Berry, so a plan built on poisoning it doesn't work — but the second status sticks, and the planner tracks that the berry is gone.
  • Sand Their side: Spikes x2 Weather, hazards and screens These belong to the side, not the Pokémon, and persist down the branch. Weather set by an ability on switch-in is applied too.
  • Sand (battle) Standing weather 235 of the game's fights already have weather up before anyone moves — sun, rain, sand, hail or fog — taken from the game's own data rather than set by you. Roark's gym is in sand, which is the entire point of his Gible's Sand Veil. A weather move or ability still overrides it.
  • mixed state Mixed state This turn is reachable by more than one branch, and they don't all leave the same boosts and hazards behind. The card shows the first; the flag is there so you know not to trust it blindly.
  • Status locked Status locked Appears when your Pokémon already carries a status and the trainer's AI leads with status and disruption. It means their harassment has nothing to land.

Abilities

27 abilities are modelled: status protection (Limber, Insomnia, Immunity, Water Veil, Magma Armor, Vital Spirit, Leaf Guard, Own Tempo), stat-drop protection (Clear Body, White Smoke, Hyper Cutter, Keen Eye), weather setters (Drizzle, Drought, Sand Stream, Snow Warning), trapping (Shadow Tag, Arena Trap, Magnet Pull), the absorb abilities (Volt Absorb, Water Absorb, Dry Skin, Motor Drive), plus Intimidate, Natural Cure, Rough Skin and Aftermath. Those are underlined on the card.

The last two are the ones that bill you. Rough Skin takes an eighth of your health for every hit that made contact — so a 2–5 hit move pays between two and five times. Aftermath takes a quarter, but only if your hit was the one that killed it; if the roll decides whether it died, only the bad end of your health bar pays. Magic Guard pays neither, and a non-contact move avoids both.

Everything else — abilities that only change damage — is deliberately left out. The damage calculator already implements them, and modelling them twice would only let the two drift apart. So are the 30% contact abilities like Static and Flame Body, for the same reason a 10% burn is: a plan can't rest on them.

Switching, and the moves that punish it

Pursuit catches a Pokémon on the way out: if the slot it's aimed at is switching this turn, the move resolves before the switch, so it hits whoever is leaving rather than whoever arrives — at double power. Worth knowing that in Platinum Kaizo Rage is a second Pursuit, word for word; it has nothing to do with the Attack-raising move of the base game. Between them that's 198 of the game's trainer sets.

U-turn and Baton Pass land their damage and then take their user off the field. That isn't optional, so the card asks who comes in: an arrow button appears under the moves whenever you pick one of them, and it stays flagged until you answer.

Bruiser Bruiser

It's a different question from the Switch button beside it. That one is "switch instead of attacking"; this one is "the move already happened, and it's taken this Pokémon off the field".

The switch happens inside the turn order, which is the whole point of the move. If your U-turn user is faster, it leaves before the reply comes — and whoever you brought in is the one that takes it. A slower Karate Chop aimed at the Pokémon that U-turned away lands on the newcomer instead, calculated against its typing and defences. Hazards and abilities like Intimidate land at that moment too, in time to affect the moves still to come. If the move never goes off — outsped and killed, flinched, Protected against — nobody moves at all.

Baton Pass hands its work over instead of dropping it — stat changes, Substitute and the volatiles all move to whoever comes in, which is the whole reason to use it over a plain switch. Two things that catch people out: a Perish Song count goes across too (you're handing a teammate the faint, not escaping it), and so does a Mean Look — whoever you pass to arrives already trapped, and their Switch button will say so. A binding move like Wrap doesn't pass; it just ends.

Roar and Whirlwind drag the other side out instead. Which Pokémon arrives is random, so the planner won't pick — the same arrow button appears on their column, in italic, for you to record which one it was. What's certain is what the departing Pokémon takes with it: its boosts and its volatiles are gone.

Both are −6 priority in this game, so they almost always go last and the attack happens first. The planner does deny an interrupted move when a Roar genuinely resolves before it, but in practice that only comes up against another −6 move.

Moves that do nothing, and moves that help

A move that can't touch its target shows 0 rather than its base power — a Shadow Ball into a Normal type, a Ground move into a Levitate Pokémon, Poison into a Steel type. And a move the target absorbs shows what it heals instead: Volt Absorb, Water Absorb and Dry Skin all take a quarter of their health back off the matching type, so attacking into one is worse than doing nothing at all.

Psyduck Water Pulse +24 Shadow Ball 0 Psybeam 72–88 Disable —

The dash still means something different: no number here, which is what a status move shows. A 0 is a real zero, and a + figure is the only one on a card that runs the wrong way — it keeps full weight and takes the colour of whoever it's helping. Nothing comes back off the attacker either: a move that dealt nothing recoils nothing, drains nothing, and doesn't pay a Life Orb.

Held items that go off

A Sitrus Berry, an Oran Berry or a Berry Juice heals its holder once its health drops below half, and the pinch berries raise a stat below a quarter. The planner applies those, and marks the item spent — a berry that's gone is exactly why a later turn stops working. It only claims the trigger when the whole health bar is under the line, though: while the bar straddles it, whether the berry fired is a coin flip.

So there's a button. Each Pokémon's item shows as a small pip under its health bar, and one click says it went off on this turn.

Pachirisu 42–56% Pachirisu 77–91% Pachirisu 77–91% Berry Juice

Left, the bar straddles half so nothing is claimed. Middle, you've said the berry went off and the plan is derived from there — it's a statement, like everything else you set on a turn, so it works even from full health. Right, on a later turn, it's gone.

Toxic Orb and Flame Orb status their holder at the end of every turn, so the status lands now and starts costing health next turn. They're never used up — that's the whole point of a Guts or Poison Heal set. An ability that refuses the status still refuses it, and a Pokémon already statused can't be given another.

The type-resist berries — Yache, Chople, Occa, Rindo and the rest — halve one super-effective hit of their type and are then gone. The halving was always shown; what's new is that they're now spent, so the second hit of that type lands in full instead of being halved again for the rest of the fight.

Only a berry with a health threshold gets the button, because it's the only trigger the planner won't guess at. Everything else fires on its own and has nothing to decide: a Focus Sash catches a particular hit as it lands, a resist berry is spent by whoever attacks into it, and an orb goes off whether you want it to or not. A Focus Band is a flat 10% chance to live, so it's left out for the same reason a 10% burn is, and items that only change damage — Life Orb, the Choice items — are the damage calculator's job.

Moves that refuse the turn

Protect and Detect stop a move outright — no damage, no status, no stat drop. What they can stop comes from the game's own move data, so Stealth Rock, Swords Dance, Perish Song and Rain Dance all go through them untouched. Endure is the other shape: the hit lands in full and only the faint is refused, so it holds on at 1 HP — and end-of-turn damage still finishes the job, which in Roark's sand it will.

A move stopped this way keeps its figure on the card, struck through, the same as one denied any other way. Hovering it says who is doing the stopping.

Aipom Slash 48–56 Swift 30–36

Follow Me pulls every single-target move the other side throws onto the Pokémon using it, whatever it was aimed at. Spread moves aren't affected — an Earthquake was always hitting both. In a double the aim row shows where the move really lands rather than where you pointed it, and the damage figures are recalculated against that Pokémon, since it has different typing and different defences.

→
All four are +3 priority in this game, which is why they work at all — they go before nearly everything. The one thing that beats them is something faster in the same bracket, and the planner reads that off the speeds rather than assuming.

The one uncertainty here is real. The first Protect always works; each one straight after it halves the chance. The planner still applies it — that's what your plan says happens — but it tells you that you're gambling, because a blocked move looks exactly the same whether the block was guaranteed or a coin flip.

That's a fork, and forks belong on branches. Draw the branch, and on the arm where the Protect failed set Move didn't go off on the Protecting slot — that's what "its move never went off" means, and the attack lands normally. It also resets the counter, so the next Protect is guaranteed again. Protect, Detect and Endure share the one counter, switching out resets it, and alternating Protect with an attack keeps every one of them guaranteed.

This is the only warning in the planner that fires on something possible. Every other one fires on something that can't happen at all. It's here because there'd otherwise be nothing on the card to tell you the difference.

Battle formats

The format isn't something you configure — it's read from the game's own data for the trainer you picked. 190 of the game's 495 trainer fights aren't plain singles.

  • Single one slot per side The ordinary case. Only the first slot is used, so the card stays narrow.
  • True double 2v2, one trainer The card grows a second slot per side. Spread moves hit both opposing slots; everything else hits across.
  • Two trainers 2v2, two trainers Same shape, but the two opposing slots have separate parties and separate AI flags, so both are labelled.
  • Tag you plus an AI ally Your second slot is somebody you don't command. It renders dimmed and marked (AI), and its move reads as likely — a prediction, not an instruction.

Three fights worth trying if you want to see each format: Twins Liv & Liz (true double), Cyclist Axel (two trainers), Galactic Luna #1 (tag, with Dawn as your partner).

Easy to miss

Everything on this page that isn't discoverable by looking at the screen.

  • Drag a Pokémon onto empty canvas Starts a new turn there, with it already in.
  • Drag the blue nub onto empty canvas Creates the next turn there, already branched from this one.
  • Click an arrow's label Edits the branch — and it's the only way to delete one.
  • Click one of their moves Pins the threat you're planning around. Click it again to unpin.
  • Click the item on your own card Changes what it holds, writing back to the Box.
  • Right-click a slot Empties it, without opening the turn editor.
  • "Ends on this turn" In the turn editor: how a sleep or freeze is closed out.
  • Fold Hides only what's reachable exclusively through that turn.
  • Drag one of yours onto another The two trade places in your team.
  • Drag a team member into the Box Benches it. Drag it back out to bring it along.

Not built yet

So you know what's missing on purpose rather than reporting it:

  • The four two-turn moves — SolarBeam, Fly, Razor Wind and Shadow Force. They're shown and can be planned, but the planner treats them as landing on the turn you pick them rather than spending a turn charging first. Nor does it model the semi-invulnerable turn Fly and Shadow Force spend out of reach, which eleven moves in this game specifically say they can still hit.

    Platinum Kaizo rewrote most of the rest: Dig, Dive, Sky Attack and Bounce all land in one turn here, Hyper Beam and Giga Impact recoil instead of recharging, and Skull Bash is gone. Those need nothing.
  • Custap, Micle, Lansat and Starf berries. The four pinch berries whose effect lands somewhere the planner has no home for — moving first in the bracket on a later turn, accuracy, critical hit ratio, and a random stat. Every other pinch berry is modelled.
  • Renaming or duplicating a line, and duplicating a turn. A line is named after its trainer, so "Roark, safe" and "Roark, risky" both just read "Leader Roark" for now.
  • Line notes, export/import, and auto-layout. Folding hides mess; it doesn't arrange it.
  • History across attempts. A line reads live from the Box, so it doesn't survive a reset as a record of what you actually did.
  • Type effectiveness on the card, level-cap warnings, canvas zoom, keyboard shortcuts.
Feedback is wanted on whether the shape of this is right — turns as a graph, state derived rather than typed in, both movesets on the card. Those are the decisions that are expensive to change later.
